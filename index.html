1. Introduction

1.1 Background on Industrial Vibration Monitoring

Industrial vibration monitoring plays a crucial role in many industries, from marine, oil and gas, manufacturing industries, to space and aircraft industries (Mohd Ghazali, Mohamad Hazwan, Rahiman, & Wan, 2021).

Reliability and efficiency of these machines are of utmost importance for continuous, uninterrupted running which minimises future maintenance costs. One of the most widely used modern techniques for condition monitoring is through vibration analysis, allowing for early detection of mechanical faults in rotating machinery such as, imbalance, misalignment and bearing failures.

The current vibration monitoring systems in the market are often expensive and requires specialised equipment and expertise. However, this limits accessibility especially for small medium enterprises. Through technological evolution, embedded systems and low-cost computing platforms such as Raspberry Pi 5 (RPi5), create an opportunity to develop affordable yet effective monitoring systems.

1.2 Problem Statement

Despite adopting vibration-based condition monitoring systems, there are many gaps in the current market solutions, such as accessibility and adaptability, particularly in marine and industrial rotating machinery systems (Chu, Nguyen, Yoo, & Wang, 2024).

In marine vessels and industrial plants, rotating machinery forms the backbone of power generation for essential critical operations. One of the most critical equipment onboard or in plants, are alternators. Alternators are the “heart” of a vessel or plant (Bright Hub Engineering, 2021). For marine alternators, supplying electrical power for navigation, propulsion support systems and onboard services. For plant alternators, supplying electrical power for plant’s internal operations depending on load requirements, ensuring stable energy output for machinery and process control systems, among other functions.

In a vessel/plant, there are multiple alternators so load can be placed on a different working alternator, should any breakdown. However, this avoids the issue, and the vessel/plant would have one less alternator, leading to a potentially dangerous scenario, where the other alternators might have issues too and breakdown. This results in downtime for the vessel/plant, and for vessel crew to be stranded at sea, as without alternators propulsion systems are disrupted.

Ultimately, alternators would have to be repaired, as using the remaining alternators while leaving one permanently down, is neither professional nor legal, due to class checks and audits for vessel/plants (Daya & Lazakis, 2024). However, cost of repair for alternators are expensive, due to extensive work required (E. Koh, personal communication, March 2026). While repair and transportation are costly, downtime costs vessels/plants the greatest (Daya & Lazakis, 2024; Jardine, Lin, & Banjevic, 2006). Unplanned downtimes cost Global 2000 $400 billion annually (Arda, 2026). In this period, employees’ salaries are paid, while being unable to transport cargo (for vessels) or unable to produce goods (for plants). Such downtime disrupts supply chains and logistics, delaying delivery commitments and project deadlines. This may have a cascading effect where reputation is damaged and customers lose trust. For vessel downtime, it might affect port schedule, leading to legal implications. Vessel/plant management would want the alternator(s) to be repaired quickly, paying higher than market rate to have it repaired urgently.

With the critical issues highlighted above, come the market solution of vibration-based condition monitoring for predictive maintenance. Yet, despite availability of such systems, there are a few issues:

- High cost of commercial condition monitoring systems - Limited adaptability to different machines and environments - Lack of real-time, user-friendly visualisation tools - Requires a trained analyst to determine issues from spectrum

Furthermore, many existing solutions rely purely on threshold-based methods, which have limitations in terms of robustness and accurate prediction of errors.

Threshold-based methods are effective for detection of severe faults under stable operating conditions (Cai et al., 2026). However, in real-world environments such as in vessel/plants, operating conditions may be dynamic due to variable loads and environmental influences. In such cases, the threshold-based approach may lead to false alarms or even missed early-stage faults. The Takagi-Sugeno-Kang (TSK) fuzzy inference system enhances robustness of the device in fault detection by modelling gradual transition between steady-state and transient states, allowing for more reliable detection under varying conditions (Haykin, 2009).

3.3 Feature Extraction Techniques

Several features are extracted to characterise the vibration signal:

A) Root Mean Square (RMS): Indicates overall vibration energy. RMS is a baseline

indicator that is widely used in vibration monitoring systems. It provides a simple but effective health metric of the overall system (Viking Analytics, 2024). B) Kurtosis: Measures impulsiveness of signal, which can indicate sharp peaks in

vibration, linking to bearing or gear faults (MathWorks, n.d.). C) Dominant Frequency: Identifies primary vibration frequency for frequency spectrum

analysis via FFT. This is standard in condition monitoring, with dominant frequency trend used for diagnosis (Viking Analytics, 2024). D) Harmonic Ratio: Detects presence of harmonics for diagnosing misalignments and

mechanical looseness. This is a key feature is predictive maintenance and signal processing systems (Towards Data Science, 2023).

These features are used as inputs for both threshold-based and fuzzy classification models.

3.4 Fault Classification Models (Threshold vs Fuzzy Logic)

Fault classification in vibration-based condition monitoring can be achieved using one of the following methods: Deterministic threshold-based method or Intelligent inference model.

Threshold-based classification

This is a widely used approach in industrial monitoring systems as it is both simple and reliable. In this method, predefined, hardcoded limits are set for key vibration features such as RMS, kurtosis and harmonic ratio.

For example:

- High RMS values may indicate imbalance - Elevated kurtosis values suggest impulsive events such as bearing faults - Increased harmonic ratios could indicate misalignment

The advantage in this classification method lies in its ease of interpretability, where there are set guidelines that these values should fall under (Jardine, Lin, & Banjevic, 2006). If they are above the thresholds, it is easy to pinpoint faults that lie within the system. However, it is limited by its lack of generalisability across different machines or operating systems. Furthermore, values that lies on the borderline of these thresholds, are not flagged by the system. This may cause potential sudden breakdown of monitored equipment due to rapid degradation. To improve on its weaknesses, we can use adaptive thresholds that are dynamically learned from baseline data through machine learning.

Fuzzy Logic Classification (TSK Model)

To overcome the limitations of rigid thresholds, a TSK fuzzy inference system is implemented (Zadeh, 1996).

Fuzzy logic allows for:

- Gradual transition between fault states - Tackling uncertainty in identifying faults - Greater flexibility in automated decision-making

In the TSK model, input features, such as RMS, kurtosis, harmonic ratio, are mapped to fuzzy membership functions (e.g. low, medium, high). These memberships are then combined through a rule base to generate a weighted output representing the machine condition.

For example:

- High RMS with low harmonic content → Imbalance - High harmonic ratio → Misalignment - High kurtosis → Bearing fault

Unlike Mamdani fuzzy systems, the TSK approach produces a mathematical output via weighted average, making it computationally efficient for real-time embedded systems (Zadeh, 1996).

Hybrid Classification Approach

The AltGuard system integrates both methods into a hybrid framework:

- During transient conditions, threshold-based classification is prioritised for rapid and reliable detection. - During steady-state conditions, TSK fuzzy inference is used for more nuanced and adaptive classification.

This dual-layer approach improves overall system robustness by combining strengths of both deterministic threshold and intelligent inference methods.

3.5 Literature Review

Vibration-based condition monitoring has been extensively studied as a non-invasive method to detect faults in rotating machinery. It relies on the principle that mechanical defects produce characteristic changes in vibration signals. This can be extracted and analysed in both time and frequency domains.

Time-domain Feature Analysis

Specific time-domain features such as RMS and kurtosis are commonly used in fault detection (Viking Analytics, 2024). RMS represents the overall energy of the vibration signal and used in identifying imbalance. Kurtosis, measures impulsiveness of a signal and used to detect bearing faults due to its sensitivity to transient spikes.

Frequency-Domain Analysis

Frequency-domain analysis using FFT is a fundamental technique in vibration diagnosis. It allows the identification of dominant frequencies and harmonic components (Haykin, 2009).

For instance:

- Imbalance: Characterised by strong peak at fundamental rotational frequency (FRF) - Misalignment: Strong peak at FRF and at additional higher harmonics of 2x, 3x - Bearing defects: Broadband frequency components

Intelligent Fault Classification Methods

Fuzzy logic is a machine learning technique that is particularly useful, as it can handle uncertainty in measurements. Additionally, it is a robust and flexible way of providing interpretable rules which mimics engineering logic to guide the system in correctly classifying faults.

There are two main fuzzy inference system (FIS) models: Mamdani and TSK (Mamdani & Assilian, 1975; Takagi & Sugeno, 1985). While they are both fuzzy logic frameworks, they differ in handling outputs. TSK model is used in preference over Mamdani model as:

- Interpretability: TSK model is more mathematical and efficient, suited for analysis and adaptive models, while Mamdani is more intuitive and rule-based, which is close to human reasoning. - Complexity: TSK model is computationally lighter and faster, however, Mamdani is computationally heavier due to defuzzification.

4.2.2 ADC Selection

The ADS1115 was chosen for its:

- High resolution (16-bit), allowing precise measurement and hence, the detection of, small signal variations - Compatibility with I2C communication, simplifying integration with the RPi5 - Cost-effectiveness compared to industrial-grade ADC systems

4.2.3 Processing Unit Selection

RPi5 was selected in comparison to the Pi 4 due to:

- CPU/GPU Performance: Pi 5 uses a quad-core ARM Cortex-A76 at 2.4 GHz while the Pi 4’s Cortex-A72 at 1.6 GHz. This means that the Pi5 is roughly two to three times faster in benchmarks. High-frequency data can be acquired by the Pi 5 faster and it can deal with higher sampling rates without bottlenecks. - Memory Bandwidth: Pi 5 has LLPDDR4X RAM with higher throughput, improving multitasking and data-heavy workloads. - Storage Speed: Pi 5 supports PCIe for NVMe SSDs, offering much faster read and write speeds as compared to Pi 4’s microSD-only setup - Thermal management: Pi 5 comes with active cooling support and a power button, which helps to support continuous operation, however, Pi 4 runs hot under load. Thus, Pi 5 is better suited for 24/7 monitoring.

Despite Pi 5 costing around two times the price and consuming more power than Pi 4, the benefits that Pi 5 provides, greatly outweighs that of Pi 4, when considering the context of high-frequency vibration-based condition monitoring, that relies heavily on processing real- time data.

4.2.4 Connector Considerations

Molex connectors are considered over Dupont connectors as:

- Molex connectors are more reliable and durable. - Molex connectors produce less noise, due to sturdier connection

These connectors may be a small component of the system, however, it affects accuracy and precision. Choosing a cheaper but noisier connector such as Dupont connectors, may lead to future errors when testing actual machinery.

4.2.5 Display and User Interface

A 2.4-inch SPI TFT display was selected to display local output by the system.

A screen can present numerous benefits over a traffic-light LED arrangement. While both are able to show operating status, the screen show graphical results and presents numerical vibration data received by the sensing unit. 4.3 Enclosure and Mechanical designs

The enclosure is intended to suit marine offshore vessels/ onshore industrial plants, the device must be encased appropriately to match the environments.

Through thorough research, AltGuard’s case should be IP67 (IEC 60529). IP67 is dust-tight, preventing not only dust, but salt crystals in marine environment or fine particles in plants, from entering the device with the sensor and ADC. Contamination may lead to potential noisy artefacts that degrade the system’s accuracy.

Potential scenarios where water enters the plant/vessel, resulting in device immersion, IP67 rating prevents the interior, sensor and ADC, from coming in contact with water, thus preventing damage.

As saltwater is highly corrosive, IP67 sealed housing largely prevents ingress and prevents long-term degradation due to its corrosion-resistant material.

However, an IP67-rated sealed housing was not used in the FYP. This is due to the sensitive nature of the sensor. It should be as close as possible to the surface of the alternator to obtain the most accurate output. Commercially sold IP67-rated housings in the market are bulky and the walls are thicker for outdoor usage, which meant that the sensor would likely be unable to pick up accurate vibration readings from the alternator.

To ensure the enclosure is as waterproof as possible, I have incorporated an O-ring groove into the enclosure design to accommodate a seal between the mating surfaces. When the enclosure is closed, the compressed O-ring reduces the ingress of moisture and dust.

8.5 Discussion

The experimental results provides preliminary support for the feasibility of AltGuard as an AI-assisted condition-monitoring system. However, there were some limitations identified during testing:

Firstly, the limited number of physical test motors. Physical testing was only done on one motor, which restricts the generalisability of the results. Different motors may have varying operating conditions.

Secondly, limited repeated measurements. The benchmark exercise provides paired measurements of at the different locations. However, more readings could be taken at all three locations, before taking an average. This would increase reliability of results.

Thirdly, restrictions on sensor placement. The motor body was circular and there were not many flat surfaces to place AltGuard. Whereas AltGuard’s base was flat and required another flat surface on the motor’s body to be properly secured.

Fourthly, limited reference instrumentation. The benchmark used was a single-axis vibration meter, that was only able to give figures for vibration velocity. However, a better benchmark would be one that can provide a graph too, and that should be used in future works.

Lastly, limited physical fault testing. The tests were mainly done on the healthy motor. As no testing was done on a faulty motor to see if it can correctly identify the motor’s fault, there are is a lack of physical fault testing. This would require the opportunity in testing a faulty motor to evaluate diagnostic abilities, while the motor is running.

Overall, the results provide a good foundation for future testing and further development.

9. Conclusion and Future Work

9.1 Conclusion

The project aims to develop AltGuard, a low-cost, AI-assisted vibration-based condition- monitoring device that can diagnose early faults in marine vessel alternators. Equipped with a hybrid fault detection algorithm that combines threshold-based logic and TSK fuzzy inference, AltGuard aims to detect common faults early in alternators, such as, misalignment, imbalance and bearing fault, mitigating possibility of further machine damage.

The developed prototype integrates a tri-axial accelerometer, ADC, ESP32S3 microcontroller as well as a RPi5 to acquire, send and process vibration signals. Through a Flask-based dashboard, extracted features such as, RMS, kurtosis, dominant frequency and vibration velocity, can be visualised to provide operators with statistics about the alternator. Furthermore, the system also incorporates a hybrid classification system, combining the traditional threshold-based logic with TSK fuzzy inference to provide rapid, early diagnosis that can identify potentially devastating faults before it occurs. This saves significant repair costs for the vessel management and prevents major physical accidents arising from sudden faults. Additionally, the dashboard is able to portray an accurate FFT spectrum, which can identify peaks, supporting the fault identified through TSK inference and associated confidence level.

AltGuard was designed to be reduce water and dust ingress through O-rings between mating surfaces, mainly, the lid and enclosure, as well as, the switch and outer surface of the enclosure.

Through experimental testing, results collected were able to provide preliminary evidence of the prototype’s measurement capacity. When benchmarked against a commercial-grade vibration meter, the vibration velocity collected was slight different with the percentage difference ranging from -1.25% to +1.43%. This corresponds to an absolute percentage difference of approximately 0.97% across all six comparable measurements. These results demonstrated a close agreement between the vibration collected by AltGuard and commercial equipment.

During the next phase of testing, the vibration readings from the motor, as well as, that acquired from an online dataset of faulty motor readings, were used to evaluate the condition- monitoring functionality. Both data were processed through AltGuard’s FFT analysis and TSK classification model. The system was successful in identifying both healthy and specific faulty conditions, where the post-serviced motor with Normal condition had a TSK confidence score of 83.7%, while the selected dataset from a motor with imbalance was identified as Imbalance with a TSK confidence score of 53.9%.

In conclusion, the project achieved in developing a functional prototype that can acquire vibration data, transmit and process that data, performing frequency-domain analysis and AI- assisted condition assessment via TSK inference model. Although further testing and development is required, AltGuard establishes a foundation for developing an affordable predictive-maintenance solution for marine alternators and other rotating machinery.

9.2.1 Long-term On-site Testing

Future work should prioritise long-term deployment of AltGuard on a vessel or on-land plant, preferably for a period of at least a year. Although the TSK fuzzy inference model was trained and consequently, able to identify machine conditions during testing, further refinement can be done to improve its reliability.

Further work should be focused on reviewing the membership functions and rule weights used during the inference process. Membership parameters can also be refined by using the large and diverse collection of data from long-term onsite testing as suggested in 9.2.1. This helps train the model further to provide more meaningful and accurate diagnostic information.

9.2.3 Cybersecurity Improvements

As AltGuard supports transmission of data via wireless communications and a web-based dashboard, cybersecurity is an important valid concern for future development to protect data stored in the Pi, especially when deployed in industrial setting.

Transmission of data through ESP32S3 microcontroller to Pi via a convenient network, introduces potential cybersecurity risks, such as unauthorised access and interception of data. This could prevent successful accurate fault identification and even disrupt communication between system components.

Further development can implement security protocols such as Transport Layer Security (TLS), that encrypts data that is transmitted between the ESP32 and RPi5 (Turner, 2014). Authentication mechanisms can also be added to ensure only authorised individuals can access the system, especially since AltGuard’s system is intended to support remote monitoring over external networks.

For industrial applications, be it vessel or on-land plants, cybersecurity is of utmost importance as the system links to the wider vessel or plant communication systems and network. A cybersecurity hack via the communication between ESP32 and AltGuard could infiltrate and extract data within the wider vessel or plant network system. As such, it is

9.2.4 Integration of Thermal Monitoring

To further develop AltGuard and to completement its current diagnostic capabilities based on vibration, thermal monitoring can be integrated with AltGuard.

Temperature is an important indicator of machine condition, especially for rotating components such as bearings (Nembhard et al., 2014). Various factors may contribute to a rise in temperature, such as:

- Mechanical defects - Excessive friction - Inadequate lubrication - Abnormal loading

Monitoring temperature readings alongside vibration characteristics, would provide additional information for evaluating motor health and potentially, improving the accuracy of fault identification.

A temperature sensor, perhaps an external extension attached to the side of the enclosure, can be placed near the bearing housing to measure changes in temperature, and this information is transmitted back to the RPi5 via communication with ESP32S3 microcontroller.

The TSK inference model can also be extended to include temperature as an additional input. A possible scenario such as, an abnormal increase in temperature paired with vibration impulsiveness could provide stronger evidence of bearing-related problems, as compared to using the measurements independently to determine faults.

However, the operating conditions and location of machinery will affect ambient temperature and its load and normal thermal behaviour. Future testing would need to establish thermal baselines before evaluating relationship between thermal variations and the mechanical faults.

The integration would support a more comprehensive, multi-parameter approach to machine condition assessment, that aims to improve the accuracy and reliability of fault identification.

A2) Further information on On-Machine Processing (Edge computing)

Signal preprocessing

This is implemented in processing.py within extract_features() function:

signal = signal

np.mean(signal)

window = np.hanning(N)

windowed = signal * window

This removes DC offset from the signal and applies a Hanning window to reduce spectral leakage. The Hanning window was chosen instead of other windows as it is able to strike a balance between leakage reduction and frequency resolution, thus making it a strong choice for vibration-based condition monitoring (Harris, 1978).

Feature extraction

Feature extraction is also implemented within preprocessing.py:

rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(signal**2))

kurt = kurtosis(signal, fisher=False)

fft_vals =

np.fft.fft(windowed, n=N_fft)

freqs = np.fft.fftfreq(N_fft, 1/fs)[:N_fft//2]

From these, the system is able to compute RMS, kurtosis, dominant frequency and harmonic ratio.

RMS measures the overall energy level of the vibration signal. The higher the RMS, the more intense the vibration, which is useful for detecting changes in load, which will be covered next, or in detecting severity of vibration.

Kurtosis measures how “peaked” or “flat” the distribution is. In vibration analysis, high kurtosis indicated high impulsiveness of the occurring event, for example, bearing fault.

The bottom two codes are for FFT. “fft_vals” gives the amplitude spectrum, while “freqs” gives the frequency bins. This converts the time-domain signal into frequency domain, revealing dominant vibration frequencies, which can be used to classify faults.

Transient and Steady-state detection

Transient and steady-state detection are implemented in processing.py under detect_transient():

cv = np.std(rms_history) / mean_rms

return "Transient" if cv > cv_threshold else "Steady"

CV stands for coefficient of variation. This normalises variability relative to the average. This means that if the vibration level fluctuates greatly, this shows the machinery is in an unstable, changing transient state. Whereas, in a steady-state, it is in stable operation, where the CV should be below the threshold.

This determines whether the machine is operating in steady-state or transient conditions.

Spectral analysis was also considered to determine state of machinery, where FFT is used to detect frequency-domain changes, but it is heavier computationally. The CV method is lightweight and easy to implement, while effective for real-time monitoring (Jalilibal, Amiri, Castagliola, & Khoo, 2021).

# Rule 2: High RMS, low harmonic → Imbalance

# Rule 3: High harmonic ratio → Misalignment

rules.append((w4, 3))

The rule strengths w1 to w4, is the product of membership values.

A zero-order TSK is used as the consequent is a constant and is simpler to tune. Additionally, it is effective for classification.

numerator = sum(w * output for w, output in rules)

denominator = sum(w for w, _ in rules)

fault_index

= numerator / denominator

This gives the weighted average of the rule outputs and is a cript number between 0 and 3.

confidence = denominator / len(rules)

states = ["Normal", "Imbalance", "Misalignment", "Bearing Fault"]

final_state = states[int(round(fault_index))]

return final_state, confidence

“confidence” gives us the fraction of total possible rule activation where it measures how well the input matches the rule base.

Finally, the final_state can be deduced from the fault_index, which shows one of the states above, “normal”, “imbalance”, “misalignment” or “bearing fault”.

This approach is very useful for AltGuard as it helps to handle uncertainty and unfamiliar machinery. Additionally, it is a lightweight code, with easy computations that can run easily on the RPi5. Furthermore, the confidence score allows the system to know when it is not certain, hence overriding TSK system with threshold rules during transient states.
